# When a forced cut contains the whole weight

The full three-row conjecture holds at
$$
 N=1+1111111111111111111\cdot 2^u5^v,\qquad
 u,v\ge1,\quad u\equiv v\pmod{9746588693957115}.
$$
In particular this includes every $1+1111111111111111111\cdot10^e$. A second consequence works for any two fixed prime powers $P,Q$ of distinct bases. For every $\kappa<2\sqrt{\log 2}$, all but a fraction $O_\kappa(\exp(-\kappa\sqrt{\log P}))$ of the primes $R$ in $[P^2,2P^2]$ give a weight $1+PQR$ satisfying the conjecture, uniformly in the size of $Q$. Natural logarithms are used throughout.

## A single binomial at a three-factor predecessor

Suppose $N-1=Pt$, where $P$ is a full prime power and $t$ has exactly two distinct prime bases. By the [endpoint reduction](all_weights_endpoint_reduction.md) and the [three-factor theorem](endpoint_factors.md#when-n-1-has-three-distinct-prime-factors), an exception would have a row avoiding all three bases. Its part equal to one modulo $P$ is $x=at$, where $1\le a<P$ and $at\equiv1\bmod P$. Each of the three parts is a multiple of a unitary divisor $U\ge3$ of $N$, and the coefficient avoids the primes dividing $U$. The binomial $B=\binom N x$ divides that coefficient. Thus if every prime of $N$ divides $B$, the full conjecture holds. We will frequently get the stronger conclusion $N\mid B$.

In particular $\gcd(a,N)=1$ suffices, by $xB=N\binom{N-1}{x-1}$ and $\gcd(t,N)=1$. More generally $U\mid\gcd(a,N)$, so even $\gcd(a,N)\le2$ suffices for the conjecture. Weights with at most two distinct predecessor primes already satisfy it. [Two-factor result](all_weights_endpoint_reduction.md#consequences)

## A subgroup with power representatives

Let $b,P>1$ be coprime integers. Every member of $\langle b\rangle$ modulo $P$ has a least positive representative that is an ordinary nonnegative power of $b$ if and only if
$$
             P=(b^h-1)/d\quad\text{for some }h\ge1,\quad 1\le d<b.
$$
For the forward direction let $h$ be the first exponent with $b^h>P$. Equality to $P$ is impossible by coprimality. The next residue $b^h\bmod P$ must be $b^j$ with $0\le j<h$. Thus $P\mid b^{h-j}-1$. If $j\ge1$ this positive number is smaller than $P$, so $j=0$ and $d=(b^h-1)/P<b$. Conversely, under the displayed condition $P>b^{h-1}$ (using $P>1$), and the power residues cycle through $1,b,\ldots,b^{h-1}$.

Now take $P$ a prime power of this form. Suppose $t$ has at most two distinct prime bases, is coprime to $P$, belongs to $\langle b\rangle\bmod P$, and $\gcd(1+Pt,b)=1$. The inverse multiplier $a$ is a power of $b$, so $N\mid\binom N{at}$. This proves the conjecture at $N=1+Pt$. In particular the coprimality condition holds whenever every prime divisor of $b$ divides $t$.

For the displayed example take $b=10$, $h=19$, $d=9$. Here $P$ is prime. The orders of 10 and 2 modulo $P$ are 19 and $185185185185185185$. In the cyclic group of units the order of 2 modulo $\langle10\rangle$ is the latter order divided by its gcd with 19. Since $2^u5^v=10^v2^{u-v}$ in this group, the asserted congruence is exactly the condition for this cofactor to be in $\langle10\rangle$. The verifier gives a primality certificate using the complete factorization of $P-1$ and witness 3, and checks both orders by modular powering.

As a second application, for each Mersenne prime $P=2^h-1$ the conjecture holds at every $1+P\,2^i q^j$ with $i,j\ge1$ and $q^j\in\langle2\rangle\bmod P$. There is no upper bound on $q$. Thus every exponent $j$ works for primes $q$ in the $h$ residue classes generated by 2, and every prime $q\ne P$ works whenever $(P-1)/h$ divides $j$. For $q=P$ there are at most two predecessor bases.

For instance $P=2147483647$ and $q=P+2^{20}=2148532223$ are both prime. **All** $1+2147483647\cdot2^i\cdot2148532223^j$ satisfy the conjecture with the two positive exponents chosen independently.

## Uniform estimates from one or two cuts

Fix $r\in\{1,2\}$ and pairwise coprime integers $P_i>1$. Put $F=\prod P_i$, $B=\min P_i$. Fix any positive $D$ coprime to $F$. For positive $w$ coprime to $F$ put $N=1+FDw$ and
$$
 t_i=FDw/P_i,\qquad a_i=t_i^{-1}\bmod P_i\quad (1\le a_i<P_i),\qquad x_i=a_i t_i .
$$
The coefficient $K$ has the $r$ indicated parts $x_i$ and the last part $N-\sum x_i$. Count a failure only when the last part is positive and $N\nmid K$. For every $\eta>0$ and every $0<\kappa<2\sqrt{r\log((r+1)!)}$, the number of failures in **any** interval of $H\ge F B^\eta$ consecutive positive integers $w$ is
$$
          O_{r,\eta,\kappa}\big(H\exp(-\kappa\sqrt{\log Z})\big),
             \qquad Z=\min(B,H/F).                                      \tag{1}
$$
The bound is uniform in all $P_i,D$ and the starting point. For $r=1$ the coefficient is the single binomial above. For $r=2$, $D=1$ and $w$ a third power of a distinct prime, these are the first two parts of the unique possible CRT row. A nonpositive last part already excludes that row. The two constants approached by $\kappa$ are about 1.665 and 3.786.

For the prime-interval consequence take $r=1$, $P_1=P$, $D=Q$ and $H$ of order $P^2$, so $Z$ is of order $P$. The prime number theorem gives order $P^2/\log P$ primes in this interval. Apply (1) with a slightly larger admissible $\kappa$ to absorb $\log P$. If $R$ repeats a base, the two-factor theorem applies.

**Counting one local test.** If $K$ lacks some power of a prime $\ell$ in $N$, the decrement identity $x_i K=N K_i'$ shows $\ell\mid x_i$ for each $i$. Since $\gcd(N,FDw)=1$, this requires $\ell\mid a_i$ for every $i$. In particular $\ell<B$ and $\ell\nmid FD$. Take $m=\ell^{k+1}\le Z$. For fixed $w\bmod m$ with $\ell\mid N$, every $t_i$ is an $\ell$-adic unit. Each specified $x_i\bmod m$ then fixes $a_i\bmod m$, with at most $1+P_i/m$ choices of the actual $a_i$. For each admissible tuple of actual multipliers the inverses fix $w\bmod F$ by CRT. Each such class occurs at most $1+H/(Fm)$ times with the chosen $w\bmod m$. Also $w\mapsto N\bmod m$ is bijective.

Consequently each residue tuple $(N,x_1,\ldots,x_r)\bmod m$ with all units digits zero accounts for at most $2^{r+1}H/m^{r+1}$ inputs. In the uniform count of these tuples, the $k$ digits above the units of all $r+1$ entries are independent and uniform. This is a count of possible residues, without an independence assumption on the actual coefficients.

At a higher position whose chosen $r$ digits sum to more than the corresponding digit of $N$, the sum of the $r$ residues through that position exceeds the total residue. Thus the Legendre layer of $K$ contributes at least one. Removing the lower positions justifies the strict inequality even when some carry has already occurred. Lean checks the two-part comparison. [Formal digit inequality](InverseWeightLayers.lean)

The fraction of single digit tuples without this witness is
$$
            d_\ell=\binom{\ell+r}{r+1}/\ell^{r+1},
$$
by counting the $r$ digits and the nonnegative slack below the total digit. Conditional on $v_\ell(N)=s$ with $1\le s\le k$, the $k-s$ columns above its first nonzero digit are still independent. The conditional mass of this valuation, given the zero units digit, is at most $\ell^{1-s}$. The mass with no nonzero digit through the inspected positions is $\ell^{-k}$. For $c>1$ put $\rho_\ell=d_\ell+(1-d_\ell)/c$. If $J$ of those upper columns witness a carry, then $\mathbb{E}(c^{-J})=\rho_\ell^{k-s}$. Failure requires $J<s$. Its conditional mass among all zero-units tuples is therefore bounded by
$$
      f_\ell(k)=\ell^{-k}+\sum_{s=1}^k \ell^{1-s}c^s\rho_\ell^{\,k-s}.     \tag{2}
$$
Only a sufficient finite test is being counted here. Each tuple passing it gives enough valuation for every positive lift with the indicated parts.

**Summing the primes.** Choose $0<\alpha<\log((r+1)!)$. Since $d_\ell\to1/(r+1)!$, we may choose $c$ and then a threshold so that $\rho_\ell\le e^{-\alpha}$ and $\ell>2ce^\alpha$ above the threshold. The geometric sum gives $f_\ell(k)\ll_{r,\alpha}e^{-\alpha k}$. At each of the finitely many smaller primes choose $c>1$ sufficiently close to one that $\ell\rho_\ell>c$. Then $f_\ell(k)\ll_\ell\rho_\ell^k$ with $\rho_\ell<1$.

Take $k=\lfloor\log_\ell Z\rfloor-1$ at each $\ell\le Z$. The proportion of residue tuples with zero units in all $r+1$ positions is $\ell^{-(r+1)}$. The smaller primes contribute $O(Z^{-\delta_0})$ for some $\delta_0>0$. For any $0<\delta<r$, the contribution of the larger primes through $Z$, divided by $H$, is at most a constant times
$$
 \sum_{\ell\le Z}\ell^{-(r+1)}e^{-\alpha\log Z/\log\ell}
 \ \le\ \exp\big(-2\sqrt{(r-\delta)\alpha\log Z}\big)
             \sum_{j\ge2}j^{-1-\delta}.
$$
This uses AM–GM on $(r-\delta)\log\ell+\alpha\log Z/\log\ell$.

For $\ell>Z$ use the multiplier at $P_i=B$. At most $B/\ell$ of its values are multiples of $\ell$. Each fixes a class of $w\bmod B$, while $\ell\mid N$ fixes one class modulo $\ell$. The total is $O(H/Z+B\log(2B))$ on summing $(B/\ell)(1+H/(B\ell))$. Since $H\ge FB^\eta$, this error is absorbed in (1). Letting $\alpha$ approach $\log((r+1)!)$ and $\delta$ approach zero proves the bound.

**Simultaneous cofactors.** In the one-cut estimate the finite test for a given $P$ depends on $D$ only modulo $\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,P)$. This contains $P$, every tested layer, and every prime below $P$ in the tail test. Treat the entire tail event as a failure for this certificate. Thus the same exceptional inputs cover *all* cofactors in each one residue class. For example, outside the stated exceptional fraction of primes $R\in[P^2,2P^2]$, the conjecture holds for $1+P R q^j$ simultaneously for every prime $q>P$ and every positive $j$ divisible by $\varphi(\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,P))$. Use $D=1$ and Euler's theorem. The same prime $R$ works at every positive exponent of every prime $q\equiv1\bmod\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,P)$.

## Two explicit local certificates

For $N=1+97\cdot367w$ with $w\equiv5\bmod35599$, the two multipliers are 12 and 28. The candidate parts are $4404w,2716w,28479w+1$. The first two slopes have gcd 4. Every odd prime power in $N$ divides the coefficient by decrementing one of these parts. At odd $w$ both unshifted parts have binary valuation two, so their addition forces a carry. Thus every prime of $N$ divides the coefficient for every $w$ in the class. This certifies the full conjecture whenever $w$ is a prime power. Moreover at $w\equiv5\bmod8$ the weight has binary valuation two, and the layers 8 and 16 both contribute. Hence $N$ divides the coefficient at those inputs. At $w=5$ the two individual binomial cuts have binary valuations 1 and 0, whereas the trinomial valuation is 9.

For $P=1009$ the programs give an 848-bit modulus $M$ and a 410-bit period $L$ such that the single binomial contains $N$ whenever
$$
      t\equiv1013^e\bmod M,\qquad 1000\le e\le10999\pmod L .
$$
Here the interval condition means the residue of $e$ belongs to the indicated consecutive block. In particular take **any primes** $q,r\equiv1013\bmod M$. The conjecture holds at $1+1009q^u r^v$ for every $u,v\ge1$ with $u+v$ in that block modulo $L$. Taking $q\equiv1\bmod M$ instead allows every $u\ge1$ independently and places the block condition on $v$ alone. The classes are reduced since $1013^L\equiv1\bmod M$.

For each representative and each prime dividing $a$, the generator records enough layers to determine $v_\ell(N)$ and reach that value. The modulus combines the largest depth used at every prime with 1009. The cross-check reads the explicit integers directly, reconstructs every inverse and instead checks $\binom{N-1}{at-1}$ at factorial layers through the published modulus. It uses $at\binom{N}{at}=N\binom{N-1}{at-1}$. Both verifications cover all 10,000 classes and 7,958 cases where a prime divides both $a$ and the weight. The deepest single check needs 37 binary layers. The unrestricted three-row conjecture remains open.

There is an explicit distinct pair for the second construction. Take $q=166M+1$ and $r=1013$. The former is an 855-bit prime. The verifier certifies it using the complete factorization $q-1=2\cdot83M$ and the order witness 181.
