# Four-term gcds in bases 2 and 3

The [sparse binomial theorem](binomial_sparse_gcd.md) bounds the excess of a numerical gcd over its polynomial gcd when the prime exceeds the number of terms. For **four terms in the remaining prime bases 2 and 3**, one can still bound the excess after testing a fixed list of integer divisors. In the seven-unit reduction this is the gcd that bounds the assigned factor $A$. Both bases really do have exponential factors absent from the ordinary polynomial gcd.

Let $p=2$ or $3$, $F=X^d\pm1$, $d\ge1$, and $G=1+X^{a_2}+X^{a_3}+X^{a_4}$, with $a_j\ge0$ and repetitions allowed. Write $D=\gcd(F(p),G(p))$, $J=|\gcd_{\mathbb Q[X]}(F,G)(p)|$ using the monic gcd, and $H=\max(d,a_2,a_3,a_4)$. Set $M_2=24$, $M_3=12$. Use the nonzero rows for the prime from the table below. Permute each quadruple $(t_1,t_2,t_3,t_4)$ in every way and collect the distinct triples $\delta=(t_2-t_1,t_3-t_1,t_4-t_1)$. There are **68** triples at 2 and **26** at 3. For each form the positive integer
$$l_\delta=\gcd(2d,2a_2-\delta_2,2a_3-\delta_3,2a_4-\delta_4),\qquad W_\delta=\gcd(D/J,\ p^{M_p l_\delta/2}-1),$$
where we index the three entries by 2, 3, 4. With $V=J\max_\delta W_\delta$ we have
$$V\mid D,\qquad D/V\le8^{Q^3}p^{3H/Q}\quad(Q\ge2\text{ an integer}). \tag{1}$$
For $H\ge16$ in particular $D/V\le p^{8H^{3/4}}$. Thus an exponential excess $D/J\ge p^{\epsilon H}$ at $H^{1/4}\ge16/\epsilon$ requires $l_\delta\ge\epsilon H/M_p$ for at least one of the listed triples. The four-vector $(2d,2a_2-\delta_2,2a_3-\delta_3,2a_4-\delta_4)$ is then an integer multiple of a vector of bounded norm depending only on $\epsilon$. This places all such cases on finitely many explicit affine lines in exponent space.

## A four-term relation with unequal magnitudes

For a minimal vanishing sum of four terms $p^{b_j}\xi_j$ with rational $b_j$ and roots of unity $\xi_j$, divide by the least power of $p$. The [radical degree argument](binomial_sparse_gcd.md#separating-the-absolute-values-in-a-vanishing-sum) makes the doubled heights $t_j=2(b_j-\min b_i)$ nonnegative integers. At least two heights are zero by the nonarchimedean valuation. If a strict gap precedes $t$, the sum $A$ of the lower terms is nonzero by minimality and $A/p^{t/2}$ is an algebraic integer by the sum of the upper terms. Every conjugate of $A$ has absolute value at most $\sum_{t_j<t}p^{t_j/2}$. Taking norms gives
$$ p^{t/2}\le\sum_{t_j<t}p^{t_j/2}. $$
This determines the possible heights by elementary comparisons. With three lowest terms the largest magnitude is at most 3. With two lowest terms the next is at most 2, and a fourth strictly above it is at most 2 plus that next magnitude.

Here is a finite proof that the phase search is exhaustive. Use $\sqrt2=\zeta_8+\zeta_8^{-1}$ or $\sqrt3=\zeta_{12}+\zeta_{12}^{-1}$ with $\zeta_j=\exp(2\pi i/j)$. For the listed heights this expands the relation into at most eight roots of unity. In a minimal vanishing sum of $m$ roots of unity the order of any ratio divides the product of primes through $m$ (Mann's theorem). For completeness, normalize by one term, and let $L$ be the order generated by all remaining terms. If $q^2\mid L$, expand over conductor $L/q$ by powers of $\zeta_{q^{v_q(L)}}$ with exponents from 0 to $q-1$. The degree $q$ forces each group to vanish. By minimality all terms are in one group, impossible by the definition of $L$. If $q\mid L$ exactly once, grouping by the $q$-th root instead shows all $q$ coefficients are equal by the degree $q-1$. A missing group would again force them all to vanish. Therefore $q\le m$, proving the claim.

Partition the expanded sum into minimal vanishing subsums. On the four original terms join two whenever one subsum uses summands of both. This graph is connected: a separated component would itself give a proper vanishing sum of original terms. Every edge gives a ratio of original phases of order dividing $\operatorname{lcm}(2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7,8)=840$ in base 2, or $\operatorname{lcm}(210,12)=420$ in base 3. Connectivity gives the same bound for all ratios.

For these two orders $m$, respectively, the verifier embeds the possible phases in prime fields at $9999999241$ and $9999999661$, verifying an element $z$ of exact order $m$ and reducing $\sqrt p$ by the same cyclotomic expression as above. For the last two phases $z^a,z^b$ it looks up the unique $z^j$ making the sum $1+z^j+p^{t_3/2}z^a+p^{t_4/2}z^b$ zero modulo the chosen prime. Every complex solution survives this reduction. Each surviving tuple is then tested in $\mathbb Z[T]/\Phi_m(T)$, eliminating any spurious modular solution. This proves the counts and the bounds $M_p$ on all true phase orders.

## From phases to integer divisors

Use the same approximating direction as in the [coefficient-minor bound](binomial_sparse_gcd.md#the-remaining-integer-factor), divided by its content to make it primitive. Dividing slopes by their common content and multiplying the approximating scale by that content preserves the errors and reduces the degrees. Write $v_0,v_2,v_3,v_4$ for the direction and $v_1=a_1=0$. The specialized polynomials are $f=p^d T^{v_0}\pm1$ and $h=\sum_{j=1}^4 p^{a_j}T^{v_j}$. Their primitive gcd $P$ satisfies $D\mid cP(1)$ for an integer with part outside $p$ bounded by $8^{Q^3}p^{3H/Q}$.

A nonconstant gcd needs $v_0>0$. Any common root is $T=\xi p^{-d/v_0}$ with $\xi^{v_0}=\pm1$. Its four terms in $h$ have heights $b_j=a_j-dv_j/v_0$ independent of the root. If all height groups vanish separately, the root is captured by the polynomial $J_0(p^g T^m)$ from the [within-group differences](binomial_sparse_gcd.md#which-factors-can-occur-on-an-exponent-line), whose value at 1 divides $J$. This applies to every decomposable relation, since it splits into equal-magnitude opposite pairs. When all heights agree it also captures every root.

Any other root gives a minimal relation with unequal heights. Since $b_1=0$, its doubled heights $(2b_2,2b_3,2b_4)$ are exactly one of the listed triples $\delta$, fixed for the entire line. In this case
$$ (2d,2a_2-\delta_2,2a_3-\delta_3,2a_4-\delta_4)=(2d/v_0)(v_0,v_2,v_3,v_4). $$
Primitivity gives $2d/v_0=l_\delta$. With the phase of the original first term equal to one, the phase table yields $(\xi^{v_j})^{M_p}=1$ for all $j$, irrespective of the choice of lowest term. Also $(\xi^{v_0})^{M_p}=1$. Primitivity now implies $\xi^{M_p}=1$. Hence every such root is a zero of $p^{M_p l_\delta/2}T^{M_p}-1$.

The binomial $f$ is squarefree, so Gauss's lemma puts $P$ into the product of the within-group polynomial and this one extra binomial, as a divisor in $\mathbb Z[T]$. Accordingly $D/J$ divides $c(p^{M_p l_\delta/2}-1)$, and dividing by $W_\delta$ leaves a divisor of the part of $c$ outside $p$. If every root separates by height, $D/J$ already divides $c$. This proves (1), including constant-gcd directions. The uniform exponent follows by choosing $Q=\lfloor H^{1/4}\rfloor\ge2H^{1/4}/3$.

## Two exponential examples

At base 3 take $F=1+X^L$ and $G=1+X^{2L}+X^{4L}+X^{L+1}$ for $L\ge2$. Modulo $F$, $G$ reduces to $3-X$. Thus $J=1$, but $D=3^L+1$. At base 2 use the same $F$ and $G=1+X^{2L}+X^{4L+1}+X^{L+2}$ at even $L\ge4$. The remainder is $2+X-X^2$ modulo $F$; its possible roots are $2,-1$, neither a root of $F$. Again $J=1$ and $D=2^L+1$. Both constructions have four distinct positions. They concern the auxiliary gcd in the carry argument.
