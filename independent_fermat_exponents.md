# A free binary exponent at a Fermat-prime cofactor

For every prime $q$ and all positive integers $b,j$, the full three-row conjecture holds at
$$
                      N=1+65537\cdot2^b q^{8192j}.
$$
The binary exponent can be chosen independently of the exponent at $q$. More generally, let $P=2^m+1$ be prime with $m\ge8$. The same assertion holds at $1+P\,2^b q^{2^{m-3}j}$. It includes $P=257$ with exponent $32j$.

At $P=65537$ the result also holds with exponent $4096j$ whenever $4\nmid b$. We give further necessary conditions in the remaining case below. The previous [8192nd-power result](fermat_inverse_powers.md) treats both $1+65537t^{8192}$ and $1+2\cdot65537t^{8192}$ with two possible prime bases in $t$.

## Which cuts must occur

Let $P$ be an odd prime power, $q$ an odd prime coprime to it, $T$ a positive power of $q$, and $N=1+P2^bT$ with $b\ge1$. When the bases coincide the [two-prime predecessor theorem](all_weights_endpoint_reduction.md#consequences) suffices. At a failure the [one-numerator reduction](static_square_cofactors.md#at-most-two-possible-numerators) gives a row with a part $x2^b T$, where
$$
 1\le x<P,\qquad x2^b T\equiv1\bmod P,\qquad
 3\le U\mid\gcd(x,N)
$$
for a unitary divisor $U$ of $N$. This row avoids 2. Since $N$ is odd, $U$ is odd. Write $y=P-x$. Above the bottom $b$ binary digits, this part and its complement add as $xT+yT$ without a carry. This follows by taking the integer quotients by $2^b$ of the two parts $x2^b T,y2^b T+1$. These are consequences of the carry criterion. [Bergman, Lemma 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/0806.0607)

## Proof for every Fermat prime in the stated range

Set $E=2^{m-3}$ and $T=q^{Ej}$. Every odd square is one modulo 8, and successive squaring gives $T\equiv1\bmod2^{m-1}$. Since $P\equiv1$ at the same modulus, the low $m-1$ positions of $xT$ must give either zero or one. Among the possible positive lifts $x<P$, the only one with an odd divisor greater than one is
$$
                    x=2^{m-1}+1,\qquad y=2^{m-1}.
$$
In particular, for every prime $\ell\mid U$ the inverse condition and $N\equiv0\bmod\ell$ require
$$
             2^{b+m-1}T\equiv -1\pmod P,\qquad
             2^{b+m-1}T\equiv -1\pmod\ell. \tag{1}
$$
Indeed $x\equiv-2^{m-1}\bmod P$ and $P\equiv2^{m-1}\bmod\ell$.

Primality of $2^m+1$ requires $m$ to be a power of two, since any odd prime factor of $m$ would factor $2^m+1$. The order of 2 modulo $P$ is exactly $2m$, as $2^m\equiv-1$ and $2m$ is a power of two. Also $T^8\equiv1\bmod P$ by Fermat's theorem. Raising the first congruence of (1) to the eighth power gives $2m\mid8(b+m-1)$, forcing $b+m-1$ even because $m\ge8$.

Now $m-1$ is odd and $2^{m-1}\equiv-1\bmod\ell$. Thus the order of $2^2$ modulo $\ell$ divides the odd integer $m-1$. Also $3\mid x$ and $x>3$, so every $\ell\mid x$ is at most $x/3$ (including $\ell=3$). Hence $\ell-1<2^{m-2}$, and $v_2(\ell-1)\le m-3$. The order of $T=q^{Ej}$ modulo $\ell$ is odd, since $2^{m-3}\mid Ej$ and $\ell\nmid q$ by $\ell\mid N$. Both factors in the second congruence of (1) have odd order. Their product cannot be $-1$ modulo the odd prime $\ell$. This proves the assertion.

## The remaining conditions at half the exponent

Put $P=65537$ and $T=q^{4096j}$. At a failure,
$$
 \begin{gathered}
 x=49153=13\cdot19\cdot199,\qquad U=13,\qquad
                 4\mid b,\quad b\ge16,\quad j\text{ odd},\\
 q\equiv3\text{ or }5\bmod8,\qquad
 q\text{ is a quadratic nonresidue modulo }65537.
 \end{gathered}
$$

Here $T\equiv1\bmod 2^{14}$ and $T^{16}\equiv1\bmod P$. The roots of $X^{16}=1\bmod P$ are the powers of 4, since 4 has order 16. The inverse condition puts $x$ in the group generated by 2, whose positive representatives are $2^a$ and $P-2^a$ for $0\le a<16$. A pure power of two is impossible. Filtering the negative representatives by $x\equiv0$ or $1\bmod 2^{14}$ leaves $x=P-2^a$ for $a=14,15$, after also discarding $P-1$.

For either $a$ we have $2^{b+a}T\equiv-1\bmod P$, so $b+a$ is even because $T$ and $-1$ belong to the group generated by 4. At any odd $\ell\mid U$ the same congruence holds modulo $\ell$. Its left side is a square, so $\ell\equiv1\bmod4$. At $a=15$ the factors of $x=32769=3^2\cdot11\cdot331$ are all three modulo four. At $a=14$ only 13 survives, and it occurs once. Thus $U=13$. The fourth powers modulo 13 have order dividing three, whereas $2$ has order 12 and $-1=2^6\bmod13$. Reducing the exponents modulo four in the congruence at 13 gives $b+14\equiv2\bmod4$.

In the binary cut $2^{14}T$ has its bit at position 14 occupied. With $x=1+3\cdot2^{14}$, absence of that bit in $xT$ forces $T\equiv1+2^{14}\bmod2^{15}$. If $j$ were even or $q\equiv\pm1\bmod8$, successive squaring would give $T\equiv1\bmod2^{15}$. Also $2^{b+14}T=-1\bmod P$ with $4\mid b$ gives $T=2^{16-b-14}\bmod P$, an exponent congruent to two modulo four. Therefore $T^8=-1\bmod P$, or $q^{32768j}=-1\bmod P$. This excludes every square $q\bmod P$.

Finally the [three-factor assignment](three_factor_exponent_rectangles.md#the-same-cut-in-both-bases) gives a second part $zPT$ with $1\le z<2^b$, congruent to one modulo $2^b$. Its scale $U=13$ divides $z$ since it is coprime to $PT$. If $b\le14$ then $PT\equiv1\bmod2^b$, forcing $z=1$. Together with $4\mid b$ this proves $b\ge16$.
