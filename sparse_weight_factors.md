# Five digit units at a prime dividing the weight

Let $p\mid N$, put $a=v_p(N)$ and $P=p^a$, and let $s_p(N)$ be the base-$p$ digit sum.

* **Every weight with $s_p(N)\le5$ and $P\le200{,}000{,}000$ satisfies the full conjecture.** There is no upper limit on $N$ in this statement.
* **Two sparse prime divisors suffice at any size.** If $p,q$ are distinct primes dividing $N$ and $s_p(N),s_q(N)\le5$, the conjecture holds.
* For $s_p(N)\le4$, an exception requires $27(N-1)^2\le P^3$. For $s_p(N)\le5$, it requires $N-1<((P/3)^{3/2}+1)^2$. If $a=1$ then only $N=3p,4p,5p$ and $N=p(3p+2)$ can be exceptions.

For a prime dividing $N-1$, the [five-unit theorem](six_digit_gaps.md) already proves the conjecture without a restriction on its full power. This note treats primes dividing $N$.

## The row with a singleton

Use the [endpoint reduction](all_weights_endpoint_reduction.md) and assign the full powers of $N$ and $N-1$ to the two other rows as $UV=N$, $ST=N-1$. The row $A$ avoiding $p$ assigns the digit units of $N$ among three nonempty parts without a carry. With at most five units, at least one part is a singleton $p^i$. The factor $U\ge3$ divides every part. Therefore $U=P$ on assigning $p$ to $A$, and all other prime divisors of $N$ must be avoided by the companion $C=V(y_1,y_2,y_3)$, whose normalized parts sum to $P$.

At a predecessor power assigned to $A$, the singleton is a unit, so it has residue 1 and both other positions have residue 0. Hence $V\ge2S+1$ and $T=(PV-1)/S>2P$. The normalized companion satisfies
$$27T^2\le P^3,\qquad 2w+1\le P\ \text{for every full power }w\mid T. \tag{1}$$
The product of its three entries is divisible by $T^2$, giving the first inequality. The second counts the two positive entries divisible by $w$. Since $T>P$, each of the three positions must be exceptional for some full power in $T$, or its normalized entry would be divisible by $T$. In particular $T$ has at least three distinct prime factors.

Two singletons in $A$ give $S=1$, since both are units modulo every divisor of $N-1$. This always occurs with at most four digit units. For the two-prime theorem a row avoiding $q$ also has a singleton, a power of $q$. It cannot be $A$, since avoiding $q\mid N$ would require all its parts, including $p^i$, to be divisible by $q$. On the second row the same argument gives $U\ge2T+1$. This contradicts $T>2P$ and $U=P$.

## Five units and the exponent gaps

With exactly one singleton the other parts have two units apiece. Write the row as
$$p^i,\quad p^j(1+p^d),\quad p^l(1+p^e),$$
where $i,j,l\ge a$, $\min(i,j,l)=a$, and $d,e\ge0$. Then
$$S\mid\gcd(p^i-1,1+p^d,1+p^e). \tag{2}$$
For $S>2$ we must have $d,e>0$. Put $g=\gcd(i,d,e)$. In the cyclic subgroup generated by $p$ modulo $S$, the element $-1$ has order two. The order of $p$ is therefore $2t$, where $i/t$ is even and $d/t,e/t$ are odd. It follows that $g/t$ is odd and $p^g\equiv-1\pmod S$. In particular $i/g$ is even and $d/g,e/g$ are odd.

Hence $S\le p^g+1\le p^{i/2}+1\le\sqrt{N-1}+1$. The last bound also covers $S\le2$. By (1), for $L=(P/3)^{3/2}$ and $x=\sqrt{N-1}$ we have $x^2\le L(x+1)$, implying $x<L+1$. In particular $N<P^3$ for all $P\ge3$. When $P=2$ the required scale $U\ge3$ is already impossible.

If $a=1$, all exponent positions are now 1 or 2. In the case $S\le2$, (1) gives $N\le1+2(p/3)^{3/2}<p^2$ for $p\ge3$. All units are then at position 1. In the case $S>2$, the gap conditions force $i=2$, $j=l=d=e=1$. This gives
$$N=p(3p+2),\qquad A=(p^2,p(p+1),p(p+1)).$$
Weights $p$ and $2p$ have universal prime $p$ for $p>2$.

There is also a uniform counting consequence. Fix $0<\eta<2/3$ and a prime $p$. Among all five-unit weights with leading exponent $H\le B$, $p\mid N$ and $a\le(2/3-\eta)H$, at most $O_\eta(B^3)$ can be exceptions. The implied constant is independent of $p$. In this region $N-1\ge p^H$ and (1) exclude $S\le2$. The bound $S\le p^g+1$ gives $H<g+3a/2$, so $g>3\eta H/2$. Now $i/g,d/g,e/g$ each have only $O_\eta(1)$ choices. The remaining three parameters $g,j,l$ are at most $B$.

## Finite verification with an unbounded weight

Use the [checked interval through one billion](full_range_1b.md). At $a=1$ and $p\le200{,}000{,}000$ all the linear cases are in that interval. For $p(3p+2)$, any predecessor prime avoided by the displayed row must divide $p+1$. The base 2 is excluded by the repeated part. At an odd prime $q\mid p+1$, its full power in $N-1=(p+1)(3p-1)$ equals its power in $p+1$, because $\gcd(p+1,3p-1)\mid4$. The C++ search enumerates every prime $5\le p\le200{,}000{,}000$, assigns to $S$ all such powers whose base additions are carry-free, and applies (1). Of 11,078,935 primes, 64,663 give $S>1$ and **138** pass the size bound. The primes 2 and 3 are covered by the initial interval.

At $a\ge2$ the digit-multiset search uses $N<P^3$ and fixes one lowest unit $P$. It enumerates multisets of the remaining units, rejecting a multiplicity of $p$ or more. It includes $S=1$ whenever (1) allows it and checks every split of size $1+2+2$. For a singleton $u$ and pair sums $b,c$, equation (2) gives the preliminary bound $S\le\gcd(u-1,b,c)$. Assigning every actually avoidable full predecessor power to this row gives the weakest necessary conditions on the companion. Of **2,310,258** digit multisets above one billion at 1,864 full powers, 11,800 passed the preliminary bound and **1,837** reached the companion CRT search. The largest weight passing the preliminary bound was $80{,}317{,}123{,}696{,}948{,}701{,}822{,}976$.

A second implementation generates weights with $S\le2$ by recursive base-digit choices under (1). For $S>2$ it starts from $g$, the even quotient $i/g$ and the odd quotients $d/g,e/g$, choosing the two lower exponents last. It factors $p^g+1$, determines the full powers in $N-1$ that can belong to $S$, and tests the row using factorial floors. It examined 13,856 candidate gap rows and independently produced **the identical set of 1,837 systems**.

For all 1,975 systems combined, the independent check constructs each normalized entry $y$ separately from the conditions $y\equiv0$ or $P\pmod w$ for all full powers of $T$. Since $0<y<P<T$, only the least residues need be considered. Across all systems there are just **158 admissible single entries**. In no system can three positive entries, with repetitions allowed, sum to $P$. This excludes the companion before any higher carry condition is needed. For instance at $p=607003$, $N=1{,}105{,}359{,}140{,}033$, the row avoids the bases 263 and 577 of $S=151751$. Here $T=7284032$ yields just the two possible entries 219840 and 387163.
